# Method

## Why examination and identification are not the same process

A specimen has a hierarchical assemblage of features. Identification keys select a limited sequence of characters that efficiently separates taxa, but this is necessarily a compromise. The most discriminating character is not always the safest or most convenient character to examine next.

The method therefore separates three activities:

1. examining the specimen;
2. organising the observations into a structured account;
3. using that account to test identification hypotheses.

## Start with habitus

The first examination is of the intact specimen as a whole. Record size, shape, proportions, major structures, and conspicuous features. Shape and structure take priority over colour, although colour and pattern should be recorded before they change.

These observations place the specimen into a working group. The group may be taxonomic, such as a family, or artificial, such as `small long-legged fly`. Its purpose is to guide the next stage of examination, not to claim an identification.

## Plan by character system

The working group suggests which structures are likely to matter. A fly might require grouped observations of wings, legs, antennae, mouthparts, abdomen, and evidence of sex. A flowering plant might require leaves, stems, flowers, fruits, hairs, and underground structures.

Observations are grouped by convenience: the same orientation, magnification, preparation, or session. This reduces handling and avoids repeatedly returning to structures that were previously accessible.

## Protect information before altering the specimen

Removing or mounting a structure changes the specimen. A temporary slide may not be recoverable, and a dissected specimen cannot be returned to its intact state. Before an irreversible step, complete the useful observations available from the current state and record them as text.

For a temporary preparation, extract and record all relevant information before moving on. If later re-examination or verification is important, consider a permanent preparation first.

## Organise rather than transcribe

The durable record is not a transcript of the conversation. Just as a scientific paper presents work in a conventional explanatory order rather than the order in which every action occurred, the specimen record places observations into a biological hierarchy.

The observer may say `the feelers look like four round beads and a longer pointed bit`. The structured account can retain that original statement and add a normalised description such as `antenna with four bead-like flagellomeres and an elongate conical terminal flagellomere`.

Normalisation must clarify the observation without adding a character that was not seen. Interpretation remains separate. The proposed identity must never supply missing observations.

## Text is the primary evidence

Images support examination and allow others to inspect visible evidence, but they do not replace the written account. The record must state what was observed, how it was observed, its specimen state, and any uncertainty.

This structured text can be queried during examination, compared with external descriptions, converted into other record systems, or summarised for expert review.

## Identification remains a hypothesis until supported

The structured evidence is compared with keys and reference descriptions. Alternative taxa remain in consideration until observed characters support or contradict them. A final determination states its confidence, evidence, alternatives, and limitations.
